    When does frost form?

    Frost forms on clear, calm nights. With no clouds to hold heat and no wind to stir the air, the ground and leaves cool faster than the air 5 feet up, where forecast temperatures are measured. That is why the National Weather Service issues Frost Advisories for lows of 33 to 36°F on nights with light wind and clear skies, and Freeze Warnings when the low is 29 to 32°F. A low of 28°F or colder is a hard freeze. Cold air also drains downhill, so valleys and low corners of a yard frost first. Criteria vary a little between NWS offices, so an official alert for your area always counts for more than this page's own reading of the forecast.

    What temperature should I cover plants at?

    This page suggests covering frost-tender plants such as tomatoes, peppers, basil, beans and squash when tonight's forecast low is 36°F or colder, the top of the NWS frost range, and moving or harvesting them before a freeze near 28°F, where Iowa State Extension notes basic covers often stop working. Cool-season crops like lettuce and broccoli handle light frost, and kale and spinach handle more. Cover in the evening, let the fabric reach the ground, keep plastic off the leaves and uncover in the morning.

    Sources: NWS Charleston, WV: frost and freeze information, NWS Tallahassee: frost and freeze, NWS forecast and alert data.

    What covering can and cannot do

    Frost cloth can slow heat loss on calm cold nights. Iowa State Extension warns basic coverings often stop working as temperatures fall below roughly 28°F, especially during sustained freezing. Harvest tender vegetables or move pots when significant freezing is forecast. Remove coverings after temperatures recover.

    Clouds, wind, exposure and low-lying garden pockets matter. Weather forecasts measure air temperature, not actual leaf temperature. This tool intentionally uses a conservative 36°F protection trigger for frost-tender crops.
